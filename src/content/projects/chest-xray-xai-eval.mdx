## Context

This project forms part of my MRes Cyber Security research at the University of Wolverhampton.

The research investigates how explainable AI methods behave across convolutional neural networks and Vision Transformers for medical image classification, with a particular focus on whether model explanations are faithful, localised, and robust.

The study compares a **ResNet-50 CNN** with a **DINO-ViT-S Vision Transformer** on chest radiographs.

## Dataset

The experiments use a balanced subset of the **NIH ChestX-ray14** dataset containing **14,000 chest radiographs**:

- 7,000 Normal
- 7,000 Diseased

The dataset is formulated as a binary classification problem:

**Normal → 0**

**Diseased → 1**

The data was divided into training, validation, and held-out test sets, with **2,100 images reserved for final testing**.

Radiologist-provided bounding-box annotations were also preserved for localisation-based explainability evaluation.

## Model comparison

Two different model families were evaluated:

### ResNet-50

An ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 provides the CNN baseline.

Its convolutional architecture learns hierarchical spatial features and provides a natural target for gradient-based explanation methods such as Grad-CAM.

### DINO-ViT-S

The transformer model uses a self-supervised DINO Vision Transformer.

Instead of convolutional feature maps, the image is divided into patches and processed through transformer blocks using self-attention.

This provides a contrasting architecture for investigating whether transformer-based explanations behave differently from conventional CNN explanations.

## Explainability

The project evaluates two major explanation approaches.

### Grad-CAM

Grad-CAM uses gradients and intermediate activations to identify image regions that contributed strongly to a model prediction.

It was applied to the ResNet-50 baseline and adapted for the transformer architecture.

### Attention Rollout

Attention Rollout was used with the Vision Transformer to aggregate attention information across transformer layers and generate a CLS-to-patch explanation map.

This allowed transformer attention to be examined alongside gradient-based explanations.

## Quantitative XAI evaluation

Rather than evaluating heatmaps only through visual inspection, the project uses quantitative metrics across three dimensions.

### Faithfulness

Faithfulness measures whether regions highlighted by an explanation genuinely influence the model prediction.

The evaluation includes:

- **Insertion AUC**
- **Deletion AUC**

Insertion progressively introduces highly important regions, while deletion removes them to observe how the prediction changes.

### Localisation

Localisation evaluates whether explanations correspond to clinically annotated regions.

Metrics include:

- **Intersection over Union (IoU)**
- **Pointing Game**

These are evaluated against available radiologist bounding-box annotations.

### Robustness

Explanation stability is evaluated under image perturbations such as blur, noise, and cropping.

**Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)** is used to measure how consistently explanation maps behave after these perturbations.

## Statistical evaluation

Model and explanation results were also compared using the **Wilcoxon signed-rank test**.

This provides a non-parametric paired comparison and helps distinguish statistically significant differences from differences that may be small in practical terms.

## Composite Trustworthiness Index

To provide a combined view of explanation quality, the project explores a **Composite Trustworthiness Index (CTI)**.

The CTI synthesises information from:

- faithfulness
- localisation
- robustness

rather than treating a single XAI metric as sufficient evidence of explanation reliability.

The individual metrics remain available so that the composite score does not replace detailed interpretation.

## LLM clinical communication

The project also explores an LLM-based communication layer using **Gemini 2.5 Flash**.

The LLM is **not used to classify the chest X-ray**.

Instead, it receives structured information derived from the model output and explanation evaluation, including anatomical-region information and explanation-reliability indicators.

It then generates a structured plain-language report intended to make technical model outputs easier to interpret.

This component is treated as a communication experiment rather than a diagnostic system, with hallucination risk and clinical safety considered as important limitations.

## What I learned

This project strengthened my practical understanding of the complete machine-learning research workflow, including:

- medical-image preprocessing
- PyTorch datasets and data loaders
- transfer learning and model fine-tuning
- CNN and Vision Transformer architectures
- Grad-CAM
- transformer attention
- quantitative XAI evaluation
- statistical hypothesis testing
- experiment design
- model-comparison methodology
- communicating technical results to non-specialist audiences

It also reinforced that visually convincing AI explanations are not necessarily trustworthy explanations and should be evaluated quantitatively.

## Limitations

The project should not be interpreted as a clinical diagnostic system.

Important limitations include:

- binary Normal/Diseased classification rather than pathology-specific diagnosis
- limited localisation annotations compared with the full classification dataset
- dependence on the selected XAI methods and evaluation metrics
- potential instability in transformer attention explanations
- limitations of composite trustworthiness scoring
- hallucination and interpretation risks when using an LLM communication layer
- no clinical-user study validating whether the generated reports improve clinician understanding

These limitations are important when interpreting the research results.
